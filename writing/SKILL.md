# Write so the reader understands without effort

The reader's attention is limited. Every sentence must help them understand, decide, or act.

## Put the reader's needs first

- Open with the conclusion or request. The reader should grasp the main point from the first one or two sentences.
- Follow with the information the reader needs to understand or decide. When there are several points, show how they fit together before going into detail.
- If there is a next step or a decision to make, make it clear. Do not add a closing just to repeat the opening.

## One point per paragraph

- Give each paragraph a single point and state it in the first sentence.
- Reading only the first sentence of each paragraph should carry the whole argument.
- Keep the structure shallow. Use headings and nesting only when they help the reader.

## Do not start what the reader cannot follow

- Introduce a term through its role before relying on it. Internal names explain little to someone outside the team.
- Do not open with side issues, methods, or history. Bring them in only after the reader knows why they matter.

## Keep only what earns its place

- Remove a sentence if losing it would not change the reader's understanding, decision, or action.
- Say each thing once.

## Answer "so what?" before it is asked

- Connect each fact to what it means for the reader: its impact, the verdict, or the next step.
- If the meaning is obvious, do not add it.

## Distinguish facts from judgments

- State verified facts and settled decisions plainly. Do not invent facts.
- Mark inferences, evaluations, and proposals as such and explain their basis so readers can assess them. Address the issue rather than judging people or teams.

## Leave room for the other person's perspective

- When asking someone to do something, make the request and any requirements or deadlines clear while leaving room for them to flag constraints. Do not make a requirement sound optional.
- When disagreeing, explain what concerns you and why, and give the other person room to offer a different reading.
- When acknowledgment helps, make it specific to the person's actual input, effort, or circumstances. Skip generic courtesy.

## Precedence

- Follow the global instructions for length and the user's tone and style instructions.
- When a task-specific skill prescribes order, format, or length, that skill takes precedence.
